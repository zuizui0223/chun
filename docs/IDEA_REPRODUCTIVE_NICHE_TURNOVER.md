# Idea: flower-colour evolution as reproductive/sensory niche turnover

## Origin: a chun-generated problem

This hypothesis is not taken from a source paper's future-work section. It is generated by a mismatch in the project's own analyses:

1. *Camellia* pigment deployment is repeatedly accessible at the molecular scale (8/8 regulatory/flux independence clusters; 6/6 informative red-direction anthocyanin clusters).
2. Provenance-aware genus-scale visible A versus W thermal comparisons are null.
3. A is not more climatically displaced than W within the two shared traditional-section history proxies in the current section-relative niche-displacement screen.
4. Yet the same visible red state can encode different pollinator/sensory functions in *C. japonica* and *C. rusticana*.

This generates the question:

> **Are recurrent flower-colour changes in Camellia more often shifts in reproductive/sensory niche than innovations in abiotic climatic niche?**

## Hypothesis — reproductive/sensory niche turnover

Accessible pigment-network changes alter pollinator-perceived spectral phenotype, flowering signal or correlated reward traits. These changes can modify effective pollinator use and pollen-transfer structure even when the lineage remains within broadly similar climatic space.

Conceptually:

`molecular accessibility -> spectral/pigment phenotype -> pollinator/reproductive niche turnover -> assortative pollen transfer / persistence`

This does not require a large abiotic climatic niche shift on every colour-transition branch.

## Competing alternatives

### R0 — climatic-niche innovation
Pigment change is primarily associated with expansion into new thermal/light/water niches.

### R1 — reproductive/sensory niche turnover
Pigment change is primarily associated with pollinator/phenology/sensory change, with weaker climatic displacement.

### R2 — climate-mediated reproductive turnover
Climate/season changes pollinator reliability or composition; reproductive-niche turnover mediates pigment selection.

### R3 — dual niche shift
Pigment changes align with both abiotic and reproductive niche shifts.

### R4 — historical/neutral recurrence
Neither ecological axis reliably aligns after phylogeny, geography and reticulation are controlled.

## Preliminary chun evidence

### Climate side

Current provenance-aware A/W thermal analyses are non-significant. The coarse shared-section niche-displacement screen is also non-significant and does not show greater A climatic novelty.

### Reproductive/sensory side

The current 8-taxon latent-state seed contains a direct same-hue collision: red *C. japonica* is bird-associated whereas red *C. rusticana* is insect/bee-associated with bee-relevant UV/fluorescence. Yellow taxa also show differing bird/bee weighting and seasonal pollinator use.

This is not yet a genus-wide pollinator meta-analysis. It is sufficient to reject the deterministic mapping `visible hue -> one ecological function` and to motivate a branch-level comparison of climatic versus reproductive niche turnover.

## Decisive analysis

For every well-supported flower-pigment transition branch:

1. estimate pigment/spectral state change with uncertainty;
2. estimate abiotic niche displacement with provenance/estimator uncertainty;
3. estimate pollinator-regime, phenology and reward-state change where evidence exists;
4. compare branch-event alignment with matched non-transition branches;
5. classify event order as climate-first, reproductive-first, pigment-first, synchronous or independent;
6. test whether pigment transitions predict reproductive-niche displacement more consistently than climatic-niche displacement.

## Minimum quantitative comparison

On the same admitted nuclear branches compare standardized evidence for:

- thermal/light/water niche displacement;
- pollinator functional-group turnover;
- flowering-season displacement;
- spectral/UV phenotype shift;
- nectar/reward shift where available.

Do not compare raw effect sizes from incompatible observation models. Each axis needs its own observation uncertainty before branch-level standardization.

## Prediction if the hypothesis is correct

- visible colour alone remains a weak climate predictor;
- biochemical/spectral states outperform visible hue for pollinator prediction;
- pigment-transition branches show stronger coupling to reproductive/sensory events than to climatic centroids or range tails;
- some climate changes affect flower colour indirectly through pollinator reliability/seasonality;
- macro flower-colour diversification can contribute to reproductive isolation without requiring repeated large climatic-niche expansion.

## Claim boundary

Supported now only as a **problem generated by chun preliminary results**. The current Camellia evidence contains strong case-level sensory/pollinator contrasts but too few independent taxa with standardized spectra and effective-pollination measurements for a genus-wide causal conclusion.
