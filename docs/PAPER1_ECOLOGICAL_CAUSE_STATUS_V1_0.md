# Paper 1 ecological cause-status companion v1.0

## Role

This is a non-blocking interpretation companion for Paper 1. It does not change science v0.2.2, framing v0.3.4, AJB bundle v1.0, any figure input, any accepted historical transition event, or the Paper 1 scientific-closure decision.

Its purpose is to answer one biological question without overclaiming:

> **If molecularly accessible flower-colour states are more diverse than the states realised through Camellia history, what ecological explanations are currently disfavoured, and what class of filtering remains most plausible?**

## 1. Direct annual-climate -> coarse visible hue is disfavoured

The frozen climate screens do not support a universal direct mapping from coarse A/W visible colour to annual thermal niche. In the original Paper 1 ecological screen, different-colour A-W pairs were not more thermally divergent than same-colour pairs within the historical backgrounds that could be compared. Subsequent repository sensitivities strengthened the same negative conclusion, but they are not required for the Paper 1 claim.

Interpretation:

- do not use `cold climate -> anthocyanin-like visible colour` as the genus-wide ultimate explanation;
- this does not exclude lineage-specific abiotic effects, flowering-window temperature, radiation, drought, or physiological functions of pigments;
- it specifically rejects a simple universal coarse-hue/annual-climate rule as sufficient for the current Camellia pattern.

## 2. Coarse human-visible hue is not a sufficient pollination state

Camellia provides direct same-hue functional collisions. Visibly red C. japonica and C. rusticana differ strongly in pollinator-visible signalling and pollinator use. C. petelotii receives substantial service from both birds and bees despite its golden visible phenotype.

Interpretation:

- `red / white / yellow -> one fixed pollination syndrome` is too coarse;
- the ecologically relevant floral state can include UV reflectance, fluorescence, reward, phenology and other sensory or functional traits that are not captured by human-visible hue alone;
- therefore failure of a coarse hue-pollinator association does not imply absence of pollinator-mediated selection.

## 3. Pollinator-mediated filtering remains the strongest ecological candidate class

Across the admitted Camellia literature, three links have independent support:

1. flowering-window environment / disturbance / weather can change pollinator availability, movement or reliability;
2. floral sensory state can alter pollinator response even within the same coarse visible hue;
3. pollinator access or service can strongly affect fruit or seed production.

The strongest mechanistic examples are valuable because they intervene on, or closely resolve, intermediate ecological steps rather than merely correlate colour with environment. For example:

- C. japonica field work on Miyakejima links disturbance to bird movement and pollen-flow consequences;
- pollinator-exclusion work in C. japonica shows that removing bird access sharply reduces reproductive success;
- C. petelotii links weather-dependent pollinator reliability with strong bird contribution to reproduction;
- Mori et al. show that two visibly red Camellia species can present very different pollinator-visible signal states and recruit different pollinator groups.

These studies demonstrate that ecological filtering is biologically real in Camellia, but they do not identify the cause of a particular accepted-species historical colour-transition branch.

## 4. Current causal architecture

The most defensible working architecture is:

`molecular accessibility`

`-> latent pigment / UV / fluorescence / reward phenotype`

`-> flowering-window environment x pollinator availability/effectiveness`

`-> reproductive success`

`-> differential establishment / persistence`

`-> realised phylogenetic colour pattern`

This is a working ecological explanation for the accessibility-realisation gap, not a reconstructed historical cause.

## 5. What Paper 1 can and cannot say

### Supported interpretation

Paper 1 can say that molecular accessibility and realised persistence are different quantities, that a simple direct annual-climate explanation is not sufficient at the available genus-level resolution, and that existing Camellia ecology makes context-dependent pollinator-mediated filtering a biologically plausible persistence mechanism.

### Not supported

Paper 1 cannot say that:

- birds caused a specific white-to-red historical transition;
- bees caused a specific yellow or red lineage to originate;
- one coarse visible hue is a universal pollination syndrome;
- the accepted-species reconstruction identifies a robust event set onto which ecological causes can be mapped.

## 6. Why the good empirical examples matter

The strongest ecological examples are not strong because they show a coloured flower next to a particular pollinator. They are strong because they resolve a causal step:

`environment -> pollinator behaviour`,

`floral sensory state -> pollinator response`, or

`pollinator access -> reproductive fitness`.

That is the evidentiary standard needed to explain why some molecularly accessible floral states persist while others do not.

## Final interpretation

The current evidence therefore does **not** leave the ecological side blank. It narrows the explanation:

> **A universal direct climate-to-visible-colour rule is weak, and human-visible hue is too coarse to define reproductive function. The strongest remaining explanation is context-dependent ecological filtering through flowering-window conditions, latent floral sensory/reward traits and pollinator effectiveness. What remains unresolved is the historical assignment of that filtering to particular colour-transition events.**
